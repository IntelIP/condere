# Preserved question guides: M1, M2, M3, M4, M5, M6, M8, B1, B5
Ported verbatim from the 26-question method (commit e26d4b59). Use them as checklists, not output fields.

## M1. Who experiences the problem, and who pays to solve it?
- Decision: Choose the customer, user and buyer whose task deserves investigation.
- Evidence needed: Accounts of the task in the target segment, who performs it, who controls purchases and what triggers the need.
- Search routes:
  - Target-segment customer accounts and documented workflows.
  - Purchasing or role descriptions tied to that task; compare with the founder's proposed customer.
- Weak signals: Vendor targeting and a founder's persona describe intended customers, not demonstrated buyers.
- Completion: Identify the roles and trigger with attribution; label unverified roles as customer hypotheses and name the missing observation.
- Points to cover: segment, user, buyer, task, trigger
- Contract rule: Distinguish the user from the buyer and source-backed facts from customer hypotheses.

## M2. How painful and frequent is the problem?
- Decision: Decide whether the customer problem is important enough to test.
- Evidence needed: Concrete incidents, consequences and workarounds in the target segment. Frequency or population claims need a suitable sample, period and method.
- Search routes:
  - Firsthand customer accounts, product reviews or issue discussions describing a specific incident and consequence.
  - Relevant surveys, operational records or studies with a stated population, period and collection method.
  - Contrary accounts where the existing workaround is adequate or the problem is infrequent.
- Weak signals: A template or how-to article explains a workflow, not pain frequency. Vendor anecdotes and self-selected complaints cannot establish prevalence or measured losses.
- Completion: Attribute qualitative pain to the people or sources observed. Quantify only what the evidence measures; mark frequency, severity or losses unresolved when not established.
- Points to cover: frequency, consequences, workarounds
- Contract rule: Attribute customer pain and frequency; do not invent monetary or time losses.

## M3. Is demand established, emerging, or mostly attention?
- Decision: Distinguish attention from demonstrated demand for the customer task.
- Evidence needed: Dated evidence of adoption, actual use, purchasing or retention with the relevant population and a comparable baseline for any growth claim.
- Search routes:
  - Customer implementation accounts and documented purchasing or usage disclosures, retaining who reported them.
  - Original datasets or reports behind adoption claims; inspect the period, denominator and method.
  - Abandonment, churn, failed adoption or contrary demand evidence for the same segment.
- Weak signals: Posts, likes, searches, waitlists and repeated articles indicate attention. Vendor customer counts remain attributed claims; purchases of alternatives do not prove demand for our offering.
- Completion: Name the strongest supported signal and its scope. Report attention as attention and missing purchasing evidence as unresolved; do not turn a bounded unsuccessful search into proof of no demand.
- Points to cover: signals, period, population, counterevidence
- Contract rule: Separate discussion, adoption, purchasing and retention; trace reposts to their origin and require a comparable baseline for growth.

## M4. How do customers solve this today?
- Decision: Select relevant alternatives for the same customer task and a useful Technical comparison.
- Evidence needed: How the target customer solves the task today, including products, manual work, internal tools and doing nothing; authoritative product or repository locations.
- Search routes:
  - Customer descriptions of current workarounds and product documentation for the same task.
  - Official product and repository pages for candidate competitors; verify inclusion against the task.
- Weak signals: A generic competitor list or a popular adjacent product does not establish relevance. Advertised positioning does not establish superiority.
- Completion: Justify up to three relevant competitors and exclusions, or explain why none qualifies. Preserve sources, known versions and unresolved comparison values.
- Points to cover: alternatives, selection rationale, exclusions
- Contract rule: Include substitutes, internal work and doing nothing; justify one to three relevant competitors or explain why none qualifies.

## M5. What would make customers switch or stay?
- Decision: Identify a credible reason to switch and what may prevent switching.
- Evidence needed: Customer explanations of unmet needs, valued existing features, migration effort, procurement constraints and reasons to stay.
- Search routes:
  - Customer migration accounts, detailed reviews and issue discussions for the selected alternatives.
  - Official migration, interoperability and contract terms; look for satisfied customers and failed switches too.
- Weak signals: Feature requests and complaints do not demonstrate willingness to switch or pay. Vendor comparisons may omit strengths and switching costs.
- Completion: Attribute reasons to switch and stay to the relevant task, and separate observed migrations from proposed differentiation.
- Points to cover: unmet needs, existing strengths, switching barriers
- Contract rule: Attribute reasons to switch and stay to the selected customer task; a feature request is not a purchase commitment.

## M6. What do customers currently pay, and what might they pay us?
- Decision: Separate existing spending from a price hypothesis for the proposed offering.
- Evidence needed: Current price and billing conditions, documented customer spending, or clearly attributed statements and commitments about buying. These are distinct evidence types.
- Search routes:
  - Official pricing and terms for the relevant alternative, tier, currency, billing period and usage level.
  - Customer procurement records, spending accounts or purchase disclosures when publicly available.
  - Original customer statements or commitments about the proposed task or offering, keeping hypothetical responses separate from transactions.
- Weak signals: An advertised price is not an actual purchase. Competitor spending, stated willingness and free signups do not establish willingness to pay for our product.
- Completion: Report each supported price or spending category separately. Label our price as a hypothesis unless directly supported, and identify the customer test needed for missing purchasing evidence.
- Points to cover: published prices, billing conditions, purchase evidence, price hypothesis
- Contract rule: Separate listed prices, actual spending, stated willingness and commitments; compare currency, period, usage and tier conditions explicitly.

## M8. How do customers discover, evaluate and purchase solutions?
- Decision: Choose plausible ways to reach buyers and understand their buying process.
- Evidence needed: Where target customers discover, evaluate and purchase solutions; buying triggers, approval roles and observed timing.
- Search routes:
  - Customer buying accounts and relevant procurement guidance.
  - Segment-specific communities, marketplaces and channel records; distinguish presence from measured conversion.
- Weak signals: Audience size, ad availability and a vendor's sales funnel do not establish customer acquisition or a measured sales cycle.
- Completion: Describe a sourced buying path or an explicit channel hypothesis, with the missing observation needed to test it.
- Points to cover: channels, buying trigger, decision makers, purchase steps
- Contract rule: Attribute buyer behavior and identify unknown conversion or sales-cycle measurements without initiating outreach.

## B1. Which customer and use case should we serve first?
- Decision: Define an initial offering connected to a specific customer problem.
- Evidence needed: Reviewed customer task, problem, alternatives and the founder's constraints, including any required unresolved evidence.
- Search routes:
  - Reuse reviewed Market findings before researching additional features.
  - Inspect relevant product documentation only for a named gap in the proposed minimum offering.
- Weak signals: A feature list, competitor popularity or agent preference does not validate the proposed offering.
- Completion: Describe a bounded offering and intended outcome as a proposal, retaining the customer assumptions and required gaps that limit it.
- Points to cover: segment, use case, promised outcome, priority reason, excluded scope
- Contract rule: Make one qualified initial offering with a measurable proposed customer outcome and explicit exclusions.

## B5. Why would customers keep using and paying for it?
- Decision: Explain why customers might return and keep paying.
- Evidence needed: Recurring task frequency, repeated customer value, reasons to leave and any observed retention with its period and cohort.
- Search routes:
  - Reuse customer task and switching evidence, then inspect longitudinal usage or cancellation accounts if available.
  - Look for contrary evidence such as one-off needs, seasonal usage or workarounds that remove repeat value.
- Weak signals: Signups, satisfaction statements and initial purchases do not establish retained paying customers.
- Completion: State a retention hypothesis and an observation period, separating measured retention from reasons the offering might be used again.
- Points to cover: recurring value, use frequency, reasons to leave, retention measure
- Contract rule: Explain repeat value and an observation period; a signup is not a retained buyer.
